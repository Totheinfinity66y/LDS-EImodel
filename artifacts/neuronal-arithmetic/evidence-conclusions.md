# Evidence-first record: Neuronal arithmetic

## Source and reading status

- Source: R. Angus Silver, “Neuronal arithmetic,” *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 11 (2010), 474–489, DOI [10.1038/nrn2864](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2864).
- Supplied PDF: 16 physical pages. PDF pp. 1–13 contain the review body (journal pp. 474–486); PDF pp. 14–16 are references. The supplementary box S1 is mentioned but was not included in the supplied file. No independent experiments or code are supplied by this review.
- This is an evidence review, not a blind read: the abstract/conclusion and an earlier literature-list entry were already encountered. I rechecked the full supplied body and figure captions, especially Figures 1–8, against the page images/text. I did not independently read every cited primary paper.

## Directly reported evidence and conclusions

1. **The review’s scope.** It synthesizes biophysical mechanisms by which individual neurons transform synaptic signals, considering morphology and coding regime (PDF p. 1, journal p. 474). It contrasts sustained rate-coded inputs with sparse, temporally correlated inputs, for which a neuron may act as an integrator or coincidence detector respectively (PDF pp. 1–3, journal pp. 474–476).
2. **Operational definitions.** Figure 1 distinguishes additive shifts along input or output axes from multiplicative changes to the input–output (I–O) curve. The text states: “A change in gain alters the sensitivity of a neuron to changes in its driving inputs without affecting its selectivity or receptive field properties” (PDF p. 2, journal p. 475). Input-gain and output-gain scaling differ in whether the maximum response/dynamic range changes (Fig. 1 caption, same page).
3. **Inhibition depends on regime and background.** The review reports that a shunting conductance can have a subtractive effect during sustained firing when noise is fixed, while balanced noisy conductances or input-dependent noise can change the I–O slope; Figure 5 shows both divisive and residual subtractive components under the stated CGC dynamic-clamp conditions (PDF pp. 5–7, journal pp. 478–480). The text explicitly says that noise-related gain can increase or decrease depending on cell type and interactions with Na+ and AHP conductances (PDF p. 6, journal p. 479).
4. **Short-term depression (STD).** The review reports that STD makes the presynaptic-rate-to-conductance relation saturating and can convert inhibition-related additive shifts into predominantly multiplicative gain scaling. It reports a fourfold larger gain change than noise alone in the cited CGC work, a residual additive component, and a 4–75 ms time course in mossy-fibre/granule-cell synapses (PDF pp. 8–9, journal pp. 481–482). Figure 6 also contains a layer-5 pyramidal-cell model with 400 excitatory and 30 inhibitory synapses; the review says inhibitory modulation was largely additive without STD and largely multiplicative with STD (Fig. 6 caption, PDF p. 9, journal p. 482). These are reported results from cited work, not a new simulation in this review.
5. **Dendritic mechanisms.** The review describes location-dependent amplification by dendritic Ca2+ conductances and a cited electric-fish pyramidal-cell result in which dendritic GABAA conductance changes gain through Na+-supported back-propagating spikes/DAP/AHP interactions, while somatic inhibition gives a subtractive shift (PDF p. 10, journal p. 483). It also reports a 3–6 ms coincidence window for dendritic Na+ spikes and a roughly tenfold longer coincidence window / ~40 ms glutamate-bound “memory” for NMDAR effects (PDF pp. 10–12, journal pp. 483–485).
6. **Explicit limits.** The review says multiple interacting nonlinearities, stochastic noise, and mixed additive/multiplicative effects make unequivocal identification of the underlying biophysical mechanism difficult (PDF p. 12, journal p. 485). It also says it remains unclear how robust NMDAR spikes are under substantial in-vivo inhibition, and notes an in-vivo imaging study that found no evidence for orientation tuning segregated onto particular branches (PDF p. 12, journal p. 485). Its future-directions section calls for further in-vivo tests to identify which candidate mechanisms are used for particular computations (PDF p. 13, journal p. 486).

## Evidence boundary

The paper supports a conditional mechanism map: arithmetic effects depend on the I–O measure, coding regime, conductance/noise state, synaptic dynamics, morphology, and input location. It does not establish one universal inhibitory “gate,” nor does it directly compare a dedicated disinhibitory pathway with direct excitation. Any mapping from its gain mechanisms to pathway-specific gating or a particular neural-network implementation is analysis that requires additional evidence.
