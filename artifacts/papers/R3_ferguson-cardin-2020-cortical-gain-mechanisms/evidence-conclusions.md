## Directly reported evidence and conclusions

1. **Operational definition.** The review defines neural gain as the sensitivity of a neuron to changes in input, measurable as the slope of the input–output (I/O) relationship, and states that gain modulation allows this sensitivity to be regulated while preserving selectivity for input features (PDF p. 1). Box 1 (PDF p. 43) gives the operational distinction the rest of the paper depends on: a multiplication of the I/O curve changes slope without changing rheobase, whereas a uniform shift of the curve is additive/subtractive and changes the input needed to reach threshold, thereby changing selectivity. Box 1 also notes that divisive input modulation can produce subtractive effects through threshold non-linearities (iceberg effect).
2. **Population versus cell level.** The review reports that variations in spike threshold and in the strength of inhibitory suppression of individual neurons can translate subtractive modulation at the single-cell level into divisive modulation at the population level, or vice versa (PDF p. 6, citing auditory cortex work). This is the paper’s most consequential qualification for anyone reading “PV = division, SST = subtraction” style statements.
3. **Cellular mechanisms converging on gain.** The review organises gain-modulating mechanisms around a common set: synaptic input statistics and synaptically driven Vm fluctuations, changes in membrane conductance and depolarization, dendritic active processes and dendritic saturation, and GABAergic inhibition (PDF pp. 4–5, and Fig. 1 caption, PDF p. 47). It states that conductance and depolarization changes cause lateral/additive shifts of the I/O transfer function without changing gain, whereas temporally coincident conductance-state change and synaptic fluctuations act together on gain (PDF p. 5).
4. **Inhibition as the pivotal element.** The review states that cortical GABAergic inhibition has a crucial role in regulating gain: local application of gabazine enhances V1 responsiveness, and GABAergic inhibition adjusts response gain without altering local selectivity or input gain (PDF p. 5). It presents three main interneuron classes — PV+ (perisomatic/axonic), SST+ (dendritic), VIP+ (sparse, dendrite-targeting) — as the current organising taxonomy (PDF pp. 5–6).
5. **Conflicting early results and their stated cause.** The review attributes conflicting early results on PV+ and SST+ effects on visual response gain and tuning to differences in stimulation conditions, and notes that interneuron contributions depend on behavioural state, cellular responses, sensory regime and manipulation parameters (PDF p. 6).
6. **Manipulation artefacts.** The review states that acute (optogenetic silencing) and chronic (ablation) manipulations have considerably different effects, and that transient manipulation of a cell type can have unanticipated indirect effects on circuit dynamics, yielding mixed gain effects (PDF p. 6; Box 2, PDF p. 45). Box 2 additionally notes that PV→PYR inhibitory responses depress rapidly on repetition whereas SST→PYR responses are more sustained, so stimulation protocol can differentially engage short-term synaptic dynamics (PDF p. 45).
7. **State-dependent direction is area-dependent.** The review reports that locomotion increases visual response gain in rodent V1, whereas locomotion reduces auditory response gain in A1 through increased inhibitory interneuron activity (PDF pp. 7–8). It also distinguishes locomotion from arousal: locomotion increases excitatory and inhibitory conductance with depolarization and fluctuations, whereas air-puff arousal without locomotion decreases spontaneous firing without reducing evoked responses (PDF p. 8, and Fig. 2 caption, PDF p. 49).
8. **Neuromodulators are heterogeneous in direction.** The review reports that acetylcholine effects vary by receptor type, layer and cell class; noradrenaline reduces spontaneous firing while enhancing evoked responses; serotonin predominantly reduces gain; and little is known about dopaminergic effects on gain (PDF pp. 9–11). The author manuscript explicitly flags a conflict marker “(but see REF.199)” on the noradrenaline-evoked-response claim (PDF p. 10).
9. **Interneurons as the main route of neuromodulatory action.** The review states that a key portion of neuromodulation’s effect on gain may occur via inhibitory interneurons, detailing VIP+/SST+/PV+ receptor expression and the resulting disinhibitory chain (PDF p. 11).
10. **Functional claims and their limits.** The review links gain modulation to adaptation, improved decoding during locomotion, attractor/winner-take-all dynamics and decision robustness, and to information transmission efficiency (PDF pp. 12–13). It then states explicitly that it remains unclear precisely how single-cell gain modulation contributes to population coding read out downstream, that cortical networks may not exhibit uniform gain modulation so a decoder may receive a noisy population signal, and that the reliability and repeatability of single-neuron gain modulation is unknown (PDF p. 13).
11. **Conclusion and stated gaps.** The conclusion lists as unresolved: the network-level impact of different mechanisms, the relation between single-neuron gain and population-level information transmission, the reliability/repeatability of gain modulation, the interactions between interneuron populations, and the contribution of gain control to perception and cognition (PDF p. 13).

## Evidence boundary

The review supports a conditional mechanism map: which cellular and circuit routes produce gain changes depends on measurement scale (single cell vs population), behavioural state, stimulation regime and manipulation protocol. It does not establish a fixed mapping from interneuron class to arithmetic operation, and it does not itself present new data. Any use of it to justify a specific circuit implementation (for example “PV gives division, SST gives subtraction”) must carry the review’s own caveats about population-level inversion, state dependence and manipulation artefacts.
